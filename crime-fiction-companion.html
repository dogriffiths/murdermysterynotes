Crime Fiction.

A practical companion to the craft of mysteries and thrillers, from setting to submission.

This page summarises Rosemary Rowe’s guide to writing crime fiction, which runs from choosing a subgenre to sending a manuscript to an agent. It is written in my own words and does not replace the book, which holds the full exercises, workshop extracts and examples.

Principles

  1. Forget fixed rules, but learn the conventions. No formula guarantees success, yet most new writers sell more easily when they respect the genre’s expectations.
  2. Persevere, practise and read. A writer is someone who writes regularly, reads widely (especially the kind of book they want to write) and does not quit.
  3. Write the kind of book you like to read. Your own taste is the best guide to which subgenre suits you.
  4. Raise the stakes. The crime, usually murder, must carry heavy physical consequences for the victim, and it must drive the plot.
  5. Play fair. Everything needed to solve the crime must reach the reader at the same time as the sleuth, even if it is hidden in plain sight.
  6. Make the solution earned. Sleuths and rescuers must work, by thinking or by acting, rather than relying on luck, intuition or the supernatural. Comic crime is the main exception.
  7. Less is more. Use fewer adjectives and adverbs, shorter sentences and plain speech tags, and keep research and back story in reserve.
  8. Get the facts right. Crime readers notice errors because details may be clues, so check what you think you know.
  9. Revise, then stop and submit. Cut rather than add, do a technical edit, and send the finished book out.

The main frameworks

Four requirements of the genre

The novel is about a crime, preferably murder. The crime drives the plot. The crime will out and some form of justice follows. The author plays fair with the reader.

Mystery or thriller

Mysteries (whodunnits, howdunnits, whydunnits) are about solving a crime. Thrillers are about preventing one, and the reader knows the threat and the victim from the start.

Four channels of imagination

Readers imagine through sight, sound, feeling (including touch, smell and taste) and abstract facts or comparisons. The author says these differ between people, so use all four, especially the ones you tend to neglect.

Five registers of speech

Registers run from intimate to literary. Dialogue should mostly imitate the casual and informative registers, with a few light cues, because almost nobody speaks in formal written English.

The clothes line

The opening crime and the solution are the posts at each end. Spaced “props”, such as major incidents, low points and new characters, keep the middle from sagging.

The conjuror’s method

Hide clues the way a magician does. Use distraction (bury a clue among similar details, or follow it with action) and sleight of hand (an uncharacteristic action or a slip of knowledge).

Chapter by chapter: what to aim for

StageWhat to aim forWhat to avoid
1. GenrePick a subgenre you enjoy reading. A novel of 60,000 to 90,000 words is the usual target.A theft-only plot, a story set around banks or firms, or mixing in fantasy elements.
2. SettingsEstablish time and place fast. Use people, objects and action to evoke a scene, and add cues for every sense.Piling up adjectives or leaving readers to assume the wrong era.
3. CharactersReveal people through actions, speech, thoughts and how others react. Make the cast large enough to create suspects.Cardboard figures, characters copied from real people, or wholly evil villains.
4. DialogueMake speech part of the action, give each voice its own register and rhythm, and use plain speech tags.Telling characters things they already know, or fancy speech verbs and adverbs.
5. PlotStart with the crime or threat. Plan at least three major incidents, a few low points and useful subplots.Back story at the start, a middle with nothing happening, or an ending that arrives by chance.
6. Style and viewpointChoose one viewpoint per scene and match the style to that character. Read aloud for rhythm.An all-knowing narrator who withholds information, or showy prose.
7. CluesUse a variety of clue types and space them out. Make final explanations short and broken up.Unfair secret clues, clues forgotten after chapter one, or too many clues at once.
8. FactsResearch the background first and spot-check later. Know about half as much again as you tell.Lecturing, trusting one website, or guessing at forensics and weapons.
9. Writing and editingGet words on the page, track continuity, revise to cut, then do a technical edit.Polishing the first page forever, or leaving the finished book in a drawer.
10. PublicationFinish the novel, then approach suitable agents with a tidy package and a short synopsis.Sending part-finished work, mass mailings, or giving up after rejection.

Techniques in action

These are my own short illustrations of the book’s techniques, not extracts from it. The book’s workshop passages come from published novels and are best read in full.

Hiding a clue in plain sight

The book suggests describing a room with an adjective on nearly everything, so a poker beside the fireplace slips past. Place the clue about three-quarters of the way through the description, and end the list on something striking.

Showing character without a pen-portrait

Rather than calling a man domineering, show his wife agreeing with everything he says, then speaking confidently once he is gone. The reader draws the conclusion, and may start to suspect her.

Setting a scene in a line or two

An opening that begins with movement, such as a dog slipping under a rotting gate, raises questions straight away and hints at the place. A few chosen objects or a person in the scene can do the work of a paragraph of adjectives.

Spreading out the solution

A progress meeting among investigators lets the team sum up what is known and what is still missing. That moves some detecting earlier in the book and leaves a shorter explanation at the end.

Turning a rich vein of facts into drip-feed

If you have found many period details, use a few early to place the reader, hold the rest back, and keep one fact per paragraph. If a detail is a clue, follow it with an even more striking one.

The five-step outline

Write one sentence for the plot, expand the crime, add a sentence for each suspect, expand the sleuth’s path, then shuffle the sentences to spread information through the book. In a thriller the shuffle is unnecessary.

Cautions

  • Publishing advice is dated. The book was published in 2014. It assumes postal submissions, printed manuscripts with stamped return envelopes and a fixed list of directories. Self-publishing, e-submission and agent practices have changed, so check current guidelines before you submit.
  • Science claims are the author’s summary. The explanation of brain regions and how readers imagine scenes is simplified, and the statement that one reader in four relies on a different channel should be read as the author’s claim. Treat it as a useful writing habit rather than established science.
  • Statistics and market claims. The book cites a 2011 survey of mystery readers, claims about real-world murders and statements about newcomers’ chances of publication. Verify any figure before repeating it.
  • Examples are of their time. Some suggestions about “don’t” plots, stereotypes and police procedure reflect British genre habits of the period. Use them as starting points, then read recent crime novels in your subgenre.
  • Real-world facts. The author urges checking forensic timings, poisons, weapons and transport. Do not rely on a novel, film or single website for those details.

Glossary

Whodunnit
A mystery whose puzzle is the identity of the killer, usually with a cast of suspects.
Howdunnit
A mystery focused on how an apparently impossible or technical crime was carried out. Includes locked-room and forensic fiction.
Whydunnit
A story concerned with the psychology and motive of the killer.
Crime thriller
A story in which the reader knows the threat and the victim, and tension comes from whether it will be prevented.
Cosy
A gentle, often rural whodunnit with little on-page violence, where community tensions matter.
Police procedural
A story told through the work of a police officer, using realistic methods and often a maverick lead.
Noir and hard-boiled
Gritty, urban crime with cynical tone and frequent violence. Noir often has a loner fighting the system.
Sleuth
The character who solves the crime, who may be a professional or an amateur.
Sidekick
A companion to the sleuth who lets clues and theories be discussed with the reader.
Walk-on character
Someone who appears only for the job they do, such as a taxi driver, who needs at most one memorable trait.
Viewpoint character
The character through whose eyes and thoughts a scene is told.
Eye-of-god viewpoint
An all-knowing narrator who is not a character. The book advises using it only briefly, for effect.
Register
The level of formality and complexity of language that suits a speaker, listener and context.
Throwaway
A small piece of apparently trivial dialogue that carries a clue.
Red herring
A false trail that leads the sleuth and reader towards the wrong conclusion.
False dawn
A moment of apparent progress or rescue that later fails or proves partial.
Suspended clue
A clue recognised as important but not explainable until another step, such as a lab result, takes place.
Misdirection and misinformation
Misdirection plants a false impression. Misinformation is a character’s outright lie. Only characters may lie, never the author’s voice.
Chekhov’s gun
The principle that anything prominent introduced early must later be used.
Synopsis
A short selling summary of the novel that shows the characters, the conflicts and that a plot can be sustained.
Technical edit
A final pass that corrects spelling, punctuation and typos without changing the content.

The three habits

  • Persevere
  • Practise daily, even briefly
  • Make time to read your genre
  • Keep early scribbles for editing later

Four requirements

  1. A crime, preferably murder
  2. The crime drives the plot
  3. The crime will out; justice follows
  4. The author plays fair

Subgenres

  • Mysteries: whodunnit, howdunnit, whydunnit
  • Thrillers, including psychological
  • Specialist: exotic, historical, comic
  • Choose what you enjoy reading

Settings

  • Say when and where, fast
  • Use sight, sound, feeling and facts
  • Few adjectives, spread out
  • Short sentences; people, things, action
  • Sketch a map if you need one

Characters

  • Reveal by action, speech, thoughts, reactions
  • Walk-ons: one memorable trait
  • Keep the suspect list to about five or six
  • Give villains a human side
  • Keep descriptions consistent

Dialogue

  • It is part of the action
  • Pick a register that fits the speaker
  • Give each voice its own rhythm and vocabulary
  • Use “said”; few adverbs
  • Try the tagless test

Plot

  1. Start with the crime or threat
  2. Introduce sleuth early and killer by chapter four
  3. At least three major incidents
  4. Space low points and false dawns
  5. Add subplots and chapter-end hooks

Viewpoint

  • One viewpoint per scene
  • Two or three viewpoint characters at most
  • Match style to the thinker
  • Use eye-of-god only briefly
  • Thrillers often alternate victim and threat

Style

  • Short, crisp lines for fast action
  • Read aloud for rhythm
  • Avoid clichés; invent fresh comparisons
  • “Murder your darlings”

Clue types

  • Overt, missing object, suspended
  • Subtle, misdirection, misinformation
  • Ambiguous, logical, message
  • Mix them and space them out

Hiding a clue

  1. Bury it among details
  2. Pair it with an overt clue
  3. Follow spoken clues with action
  4. Use an uncharacteristic act or word
  5. Never let the author lie

Research

  • Background first, spot checks later
  • Know about half as much again as you tell
  • Cross-check the internet
  • Ask people, libraries, archives, museums
  • If unsure, hint rather than invent

Revise

  • Cut rather than add
  • Halve long sentences
  • Hunt adverbs and adjectives
  • Check continuity and credibility
  • Finish with a technical edit

Submit

  • Finish the novel first
  • Target agents who handle crime
  • Send three chapters, synopsis, letter, return postage
  • Keep writing the next book
